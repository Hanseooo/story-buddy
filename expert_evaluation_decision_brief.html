StoryBuddy capstone · evidence review · 24 August 2026

Use prior research carefully. Keep the method honest.

A fact-checked decision brief for researchers considering an existing questionnaire for the expert evaluation of AI-generated children's picture books—plus a complete, printable draft of StoryBuddy's qualitative expert-validation instrument.

Researcher-facing Primary sources linked Tool B pending approval No pipeline change proposed
Keep The qualitative method

Three expert validators, five pre-set criteria, open responses, directed content analysis.

Adapt Continuity concepts

Visible evidence, viewpoint tolerance, recurring characters, important props, and settings.

Do not adopt A mismatched score

Neither ContinuityEval's VLM rubric nor PropCons is a validated book-acceptability outcome.

01

What should StoryBuddy do?

Do not replace the current research design with a single newly found paper. Build a transparent chain of evidence in which each source supports only the construct it actually studied.

Recommended answer Keep.
Adapt.
Validate.

The existing qualitative method remains the strongest fit for Objective 3.

Why this is stronger than copying a questionnaire

  • It matches the claim. StoryBuddy asks whether complete books are acceptable in presentation quality and classroom suitability—not whether two cinematic frames receive the same autorater score.
  • It respects the sample. Three purposively selected validators can produce rich expert evidence, but they do not support population estimates or a psychometric scale.
  • It avoids circularity. The runtime judge helps choose images. Human expert responses—not that judge—must carry the acceptability finding.
  • It remains traceable. Every prompt maps to an accepted criterion and every borrowed concept maps to a named source.
Important distinction: “from an existing study” is not the same as “valid here.”

Exact wording improves defensibility only when the original construct, evaluated object, respondents, context, administration, and score interpretation also match StoryBuddy. Otherwise, exact copying can faithfully measure the wrong thing.

02

What kind of study is StoryBuddy?

The present label, “quantitative-developmental,” describes only part of the evidence. The study develops a software artifact and evaluates it through one qualitative and two quantitative strands. The label should tell the truth about all three.

Recommended classification Developmental R&D with a QUAN-dominant mixed-methods evaluation

“QUAN-dominant” preserves the team's quantitative emphasis. “Mixed-methods” acknowledges Objective 3's qualitative evidence. “Developmental R&D” names the building and iterative refinement of the system under study.

The decisive issue is not whether opinions are subjective

Subjective judgments can produce quantitative data when people answer fixed numeric items, as in Tool C's 5-point Likert scale. Conversely, a technically expert opinion remains qualitative when it is collected as open prose and interpreted through content analysis, as in Tool B. The data form, analysis, and intended inference determine the method—not whether a human judgment is involved.

“The study employs a quantitative-developmental research design” and evaluates the system through expert validation, judge classification, and ISO/IEC 25010 software quality.
Current wording · docs/capstone/methodology.md:24–28

That opening sentence is incomplete because the same methodology later defines Objective 3 as open-ended expert responses analysed through content analysis. Calling the whole evaluation purely quantitative would hide a real data strand rather than simplify it.

Why a purely quantitative label is not defensible under the current objectives

QUAL · Objective 03

Expert meaning, not a scale score

Three purposively selected Arts and Education validators answer open-ended questions. Their responses are segmented into meaning units and coded by criterion and orientation. Counts may summarize the coding, but counting themes does not turn the underlying interpretive evidence into a population survey.

  • No ordinal response scale
  • No aggregate acceptability score
  • No population estimate from n = 3
  • No causal or comparative inference
QUAN · Objective 04

Classification performance

The fine-tuned judge produces binary predictions on a character-disjoint held-out set. Human labels establish the reference outcome; precision, recall, F1, confidence intervals, and inter-annotator agreement produce quantitative evidence.

  • F1 is the primary reported metric
  • Bootstrap resamples by character
  • Held-out test is read once
  • Deployment gate is separate from the finding
QUAN · Objective 05

Perceived software quality

A separate evaluator group answers structured 5-point items derived from applicable ISO/IEC 25010 characteristics. Weighted means and standard deviations summarize perceived software quality—not generated-book acceptability and not model accuracy.

  • Numeric Likert responses
  • Five separate quality characteristics
  • Local questionnaire still needs validation
  • Separate respondents from Tool B
Small samples are not automatically qualitative—but they limit the claims numbers can support.

The three-person expert panel is appropriate for purposive, role-specific expert interpretation. It is not a defensible basis for percentages generalized to teachers, artists, schools, or learners. “Two of three validators” may be reported descriptively; it must not become “67% of experts” or an inferred population acceptance rate.

Why mixed methods should be used—and what must be added

Explain

QUAL answers “what and why?”

Tool B can identify which page, transition, visual feature, or classroom concern experts found successful or problematic, including disagreements that a single score would erase.

Measure

QUAN answers “how much?”

Objective 4 measures classifier performance; Objective 5 summarizes perceived system quality. These numbers are reproducible within their defined samples and instruments.

Integrate

Together answers “what does it mean?”

A final joint display should place expert themes, judge error patterns, and software-quality results beside one another by claim. Agreement and disagreement are interpreted explicitly; the strands are never collapsed into one composite score.

Integration is the missing step.

Collecting qualitative and quantitative evidence in one project is not sufficient by itself. NIH mixed-methods guidance requires intentional integration. StoryBuddy can satisfy that requirement with a predeclared joint display and an interpretation section that explains how the strands complement, contradict, or qualify one another. Integration must preserve the non-circularity rule: the runtime judge never scores Objective 3.

Three defensible design choices

Recommended

Option 1 · QUAN-dominant mixed methods

Developmental R&D with an integrated evaluation and quantitative emphasis.

  • Why: matches all five accepted objectives without pretending Tool B is numeric.
  • Keep: open-ended Tool B, F1 evaluation, ISO questionnaire, and strict separation of claims.
  • Add: one explicit integration question, joint display, and interpretation procedure.
  • Benefit: smallest defensible change and richest explanation at defense.
  • Cost: requires disciplined qualitative coding and a second coder for a predeclared subset.
Acceptable

Option 2 · Multi-method developmental evaluation

Keep qualitative and quantitative legs separate and do not claim mixed-methods integration.

  • Why: accurately describes the current documents before an integration procedure exists.
  • Benefit: minimal methodological editing and clear objective-by-objective reporting.
  • Cost: weaker synthesis; a panel may ask why the evidence streams never inform one another.
  • Use when: the adviser rejects mixed-methods framing but retains Tool B unchanged.
Not recommended now

Option 3 · Pure quantitative redesign

Replace or demote Tool B and make every reported outcome numeric.

  • Why someone may choose it: institutional preference for a single quantitative label.
  • Required change: a scored expert instrument, defensible score interpretation, revised Objective 3, new ADR, manuscript reconciliation, and likely a larger or differently composed sample.
  • Benefit: simpler design label and easier tabular summary.
  • Cost: discards explanatory evidence, creates validation work, and is the highest schedule risk before October.

03

Three evaluation legs. Three different claims.

Tool A supports Objectives 1–2, while Objective 3 uses Tool B, Objective 4 uses labelled image pairs, and Objective 5 uses Tool C. Separating these objects of evaluation is the study's main defense against overstated results.

OBJECTIVE 03 · TOOL B

Generated-book acceptability

Arts and Education validators review complete books through open-ended questions about coherence, faithfulness, presentation, consistency, and classroom suitability.

OBJECTIVE 04 · LABELLED PAIRS

Judge classification performance

The fine-tuned judge predicts same versus different character on a character-disjoint held-out set. Precision, recall, and F1 compare predictions with human reference labels.

OBJECTIVE 05 · TOOL C

Perceived software quality

A separate evaluator group rates the software using applicable ISO/IEC 25010 characteristics. This evaluates the system, not the artistic quality of its books.

Software usability ≠ book acceptability
Judge F1 ≠ expert artistic judgment
Expert feedback ≠ causal pipeline effect
The runtime judge cannot be the Objective-3 outcome.

It drives corrected retries and best-of selection. Using it to grade the resulting pages would let the system grade work it already influenced. Human expert feedback remains independent.

Where ContinuityEval already aligns with the pipeline

StoryBuddy's judge uses visible evidence, tolerates pose/viewpoint/occlusion, checks inclusive character identity axes, and evaluates visible objects and permanent setting constraints.

Where it differs

StoryBuddy compares pages with canonical references and structured scene constraints. It does not compute pairwise page-to-page FaceCons, BodyCons, background consistency, or PropCons.

05

“Can we use an exact questionnaire for validity?”

Sometimes—but only after it passes a transfer-validity test. Validity is evidence supporting a particular interpretation and use, not a permanent stamp attached to a list of questions.

Same construct?

Book acceptability, character continuity, software usability, and learning effectiveness are different constructs.

Same evaluated object?

A complete child-written picture book is not a frame transition, award submission, or generic learning object.

Same respondents?

Arts and Education experts are not interchangeable with VLM autoraters, crowd workers, children, or software testers.

Same setting?

Grade 5–6 Philippine classroom suitability differs from preschool mediation or cinematic storyboard continuity.

Same administration and score?

Open responses coded by meaning unit cannot inherit evidence from a 1–5 automated score without a new validation study.

Permitted reuse?

Citation, adaptation rights, item reproduction, translations, and score validity are separate checks.

Exact wording without construct match
≠
Valid use for StoryBuddy's claim
The defensible compromise

Preserve StoryBuddy's five accepted criteria, operationalize them as neutral open-ended questions, map each supporting concept to its source, then obtain content/face review from the adviser before administration. This is adaptation with a visible audit trail, not invention disguised as a validated scale.

Do not use this percentage as StoryBuddy's headline prop-continuity result.

It can reward subset retention, gives incidental and story-critical props equal weight, and the quoted protocol does not state how to handle a zero denominator. Instead, experts should discuss important recurring props expected in both pages and distinguish unexplained drift from story-supported transfer, damage, transformation, consumption, or absence.

What the reported human-alignment evidence actually supports
Three evaluators experienced in image generation and visual evaluation reportedly used the same transition rubrics, yielding average Fleiss' κ of 0.74. Autorater correlations are reported from 0.61 to 0.71 across background, character, and prop dimensions. This supports ContinuityEval as a promising storyboard metric. It does not validate StoryBuddy's five-criterion expert interview, its classroom-suitability claim, or a new composite book score.
Why the sample-count inconsistency must be disclosed
Appendix A.7 states that correlations were computed on 100 randomly sampled transitions; Table 14's caption states 50. Without a correction from the authors, StoryBuddy should report the discrepancy rather than silently choose one number. The correlations can be cited cautiously, but not as a fully resolved validation protocol.

09

Edge-case laboratory

The instrument needs rules for the cases most likely to create false criticism, hidden bias, or unreportable data.

CASE 01

Hairless or non-human character

A robot's display, a dragon's markings, or a vehicle's body plan may carry identity.

Rule: Mark hair not applicable; use face/interface, silhouette, colours, body features, clothing, and accessories.
CASE 02

Rear, profile, or occluded view

A feature hidden by the requested camera angle cannot be inspected.

Rule: Do not code absence unless visible evidence contradicts the canon or the story required visibility.
CASE 03

Multiple characters

Feedback such as “the shirt changed” is unusable if the target character is unclear.

Rule: Record character and page identifiers; retain separate observations for each recurring character.
CASE 04

Intentional appearance change

A costume change, growth, disguise, injury, or transformation may be story-supported.

Rule: Code inconsistency only when the change is unsupported or contradicts the source story.
CASE 05

Transferred, broken, or consumed prop

State change is not the same as identity drift.

Rule: Check story sequence and expected state; code unexplained change separately from faithful transformation.
CASE 06

No visible props

A numeric recoverability formula may divide by zero or force an invented judgment.

Rule: Record “not applicable”; do not treat it as zero consistency or perfect consistency.
CASE 07

Weather, lighting, time, or damage

The same location can look different while remaining the same place.

Rule: Judge permanent features; accept temporary changes supported by later events or conditions.
CASE 08

Lighting mistaken for colour drift

Warm or cool lighting can alter apparent colour without changing character identity.

Rule: Require a stable visible contradiction, not a plausible rendering shift.
CASE 09

Critical versus incidental prop

A story's magic key and a background cup should not silently carry equal interpretive weight.

Rule: Explain narrative importance in the qualitative finding; do not flatten both into one percentage.
CASE 10

Faithfulness without exposing PII

Experts need the story to judge fidelity, but raw child identifiers must stay hidden.

Rule: Present the anonymized, redacted source story beside the book under an opaque ID.
CASE 11

Blank or unclear response

Silence does not mean approval and does not mean criticism.

Rule: Code missing or unclear separately; never convert it into positive or negative feedback.
CASE 12

Page-level versus book-level feedback

“Inconsistent” may refer to one frame, one transition, or the whole book.

Rule: Capture page IDs and scope; report isolated and recurring findings differently.
CASE 13

Reviewer fatigue and order effects

Later books may receive shorter or harsher reviews.

Rule: Shuffle book order, record review duration, allow breaks, and keep instructions identical.
CASE 14

Discipline disagreement

An Arts validator and Education validator may reasonably prioritize different issues.

Rule: Preserve role and disagreement; do not force consensus or average unlike expertise.
CASE 15

All-positive responses

A leading form can manufacture praise and hide missing scrutiny.

Rule: Ask “what, if anything” and request page evidence; do not require a criticism.
CASE 16

One passage contains several judgments

A response can praise colour while criticizing character drift and suggesting a clearer caption.

Rule: Split into meaning units and allow multiple criterion/orientation codes.
10

Recommended evaluation procedure

A reproducible path from adviser approval to defensible reporting.

  1. Freeze the instrument.Resolve wording, roles, coding unit, privacy, and reporting rules before the first expert sees a book.
  2. Record source provenance.Maintain a private source map for each adapted concept; include citation, version, license, and change description.
  3. Prepare blinded packets.Pair each opaque book ID with its anonymized, PII-redacted source story. Remove judge results, retry history, model details, and internal file paths.
  4. Assign expertise deliberately.Arts validators focus on visual/artistic presentation; the Education validator focuses on classroom suitability. All may comment elsewhere, but scope stays visible.
  5. Shuffle and pace.Randomize book order per validator, record review time, and schedule breaks to reduce fatigue and order bias.
  6. Collect page-grounded responses.Use identical neutral questions and require page identifiers when a judgment concerns a specific image or transition.
  7. Segment meaning units.Split compound answers before coding; do not assign one sentiment to an entire paragraph.
  8. Apply the codebook.Code criterion plus positive, negative, suggestion, missing, or unclear. One unit may receive more than one code.
  9. Double-code a declared subset.Use a second researcher to check the codebook's application, document disagreements, and resolve them transparently without inventing a threshold after results.
  10. Report descriptively.Show per-criterion patterns, page-grounded examples, representative anonymous excerpts, disagreements, missing responses, and limits. Do not imply population prevalence.

Researcher coding guide

The trustworthiness of open-ended evidence depends on what counts as a unit and how decisions are recorded.

Unit of analysis

A meaning unit: the smallest passage expressing one interpretable judgment. Split compound answers before coding.

PositiveThe validator identifies something working well under a criterion.
NegativeThe validator identifies a concrete problem or limitation.
SuggestionThe validator proposes a change, whether or not the passage also contains criticism.
MissingNo response was provided for the question.
UnclearA response exists but cannot be interpreted reliably or grounded to the criterion.

Required coding fields

  • Anonymous validator ID and role
  • Opaque book ID
  • Verbatim meaning unit
  • One or more of the five criteria
  • One or more orientation codes
  • Page or transition identifiers when available
  • Character, prop, setting, or presentation subject when applicable
  • Coder ID, date, and resolution note if disputed
Multiple coding is expected.

“The colours are engaging, but the dragon changes wings; keep the first design” can be positive visual presentation, negative visual consistency, and a suggestion.

How to report three validators

Report descriptive counts and patterns by criterion and validator role. Do not convert “two of three” into a population percentage, average unlike disciplines into a pseudo-score, or claim reliability from a statistic selected after seeing the results.

Evidence status at the time of this brief

No traceable source reviewed here supplies a psychometrically validated, administration-ready questionnaire that simultaneously measures AI-generated Grade 5–6 picture-book narrative coherence, source-story faithfulness, visual presentation, cross-page continuity, and Philippine classroom suitability. This is a bounded conclusion from the reviewed evidence, not a claim that no such instrument can exist.

StoryBuddy research decision: retain independent qualitative expert validation, adapt observable continuity cues with attribution, validate the final form for this context, and keep automated product gates separate from research outcomes.
